Overview

Mount Vesuvius is one of the world's most famous volcanoes, notorious for its catastrophic eruption in AD 79 that buried the Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum beneath ash and pumice. Its proximity to densely populated areas makes it one of the most closely monitored volcanoes on Earth.

The volcano consists of a large cone (Gran Cono) within the older collapsed caldera of Mount Somma. Though currently quiet, Vesuvius has erupted many times in recorded history, with the last significant eruption occurring in 1944. Today it stands as a stark reminder of volcanic power and human vulnerability.

Key Facts

⚰️ AD 79 Eruption

Rapid pyroclastic surges buried Pompeii and Herculaneum, preserving daily Roman life under meters of ash.

🏙️ High Risk Zone

Over 3 million people live near Vesuvius; evacuation plans are regularly updated by Italian authorities.

🕵️ Archaeology

Excavations continue to uncover remarkably preserved buildings, frescoes and artifacts.

🌋 Dual Structure

Modern cone sits within the older Somma rim, forming a distinctive nested caldera profile.

🔥 1944 Activity

Last significant eruption damaged villages and WWII aircraft at a nearby Allied airfield.

🔭 Monitoring

Seismic, gas and deformation sensors track early signs of unrest for civil protection.
